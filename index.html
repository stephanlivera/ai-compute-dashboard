How the gap is being filled

  • Buybacks have stopped at two of them. Alphabet (2025: $45.7B) and Meta (2025: $26.2B) bought back no stock in Jan–Sep 2026. Amazon doesn't buy back stock, and Microsoft's buybacks have slowed a little.
  • They're selling shares. Alphabet raised $42.9B of common stock and $19.1B of mandatory convertibles in Jan–Sep 2026, for "capital expenditures to scale AI infrastructure". Meta agreed a $25B forward share sale (cash on settlement by Nov 2027) and ~$12.5B of mandatory convertibles. Oracle raised ~$35B of equity across convertibles, at-the-market sales and a $10B offering.
  • And borrowing. In Jan–Sep 2026, Amazon raised $115.1B of long-term debt, Alphabet $85.2B and Meta $24.9B. Microsoft sold $25B of bonds on 29 Oct, its first in nine years. Amazon (−$66B) and Oracle (−$88B) now have net debt, and Meta is about even.
  • Off balance sheet too. Meta's Hyperion joint venture with Blue Owl (~$27B) and a ~$14B Texas venture leave it with up to $68.4B of exposure, mostly residual-value guarantees. Alphabet guarantees $48.0B of other companies' data-centre leases and power contracts, up from $16.9B at end-2025.

What the forecasts say, and the counter-case

  • Free cash flow is expected to go negative. S&P Global Ratings expects all six hyperscalers (including Oracle and SpaceX) to have negative free operating cash flow in 2026 and 2027, recovering only in 2029. BofA's estimate for eight big spenders is −$64B (2026), −$144B (2027) and −$186B (2028). Amazon's own release: free cash flow "decreased to an outflow of $42.4 billion for the trailing twelve months".
  • Microsoft is the exception. It expects to stay free-cash-flow positive in FY27, and it still has net cash.
  • Oracle has the tightest path. It targets positive free cash flow in 2H FY28 and $30B+ in FY29, with capex peaking in FY27–28 and growth "self-funded beginning in FY29" (Investor Day, 28 Oct).
  • The counter-case. Operating cash flow is still growing fast (Big 4: $677B in the latest 12 months vs $581B in 2025). The spend is backed by ~$1.8T of signed cloud backlog, and rising GPU rental rates support the 5–6-year server lives. Amazon has been here before: in 2021–22 its capex ran at 120–125% of operating cash flow during a warehouse build-out, and then it fell back.

What a gigawatt means

  • 1 GW of power runs roughly 750k–850k US homes.
  • 1 GW of AI capacity is about 500k–600k Blackwell-class GPUs, all-in.
  • All-in capex is roughly $35–50B per GW (chips, building, power).
  • ~110 GW of new AI capacity by 2030 implies ~$4–5T of build-out.

Why it matters for the build-out

  • Any given capability gets cheaper by roughly 5× to 100× a year, much faster than Moore's law. The estimates differ by method: Epoch 13×/yr (2026), MIT FutureTech 5–10×/yr, Artificial Analysis 18–123× in about a year depending on the score.
  • The cost of frontier capability does not fall this way. Every new top model resets the price bar (below).
  • Cheaper tokens drive more usage, including agents and long reasoning chains. That demand is what fills the gigawatts above.

Flagship list prices at launch, 2023–2026

$ per 1M tokens, as a 3:1 input:output blend of list prices (our calculation), log scale. Each dot is one of a lab's top models when it launched. Since 2025 launch prices have sat in a ~$3–$20 band, and OpenAI's rose four launches in a row to GPT-6 Astra's $20. Cheaper siblings follow fast: GPT-6.1 Sol matched Astra on Epoch's ECI at $4. Prices per token aren't costs per task, because newer models often use more tokens.

Cheaper per token, more tokens per task

  • Reasoning multiplies tokens. Reasoning models use ~8× the tokens of older models on the same questions, and their output length grows ~5× a year (Epoch). Claude 3.7 Sonnet used 98M output tokens to run Artificial Analysis's index with thinking on, against 6.3M with it off.
  • Better scores still cost more. The top Artificial Analysis index score rose from 21 to 58 in a year, at ~11× the cost per task (20 Oct 2026). Running frontier models is getting 3–18× more expensive a year (MIT FutureTech), even as each fixed level gets cheaper.
  • Agents are the new token sink. Agent traffic on OpenRouter grew ~14× from Feb to Aug 2026 (7-day averages), against 2.8× for human traffic (reported by The Decoder). The median OpenAI researcher's coding-agent spend went from $0 to ~$600 a day between Jan and Aug 2026 (Epoch, at list prices).
  • Efficiency gains cut both ways. At a fixed level, newer models often need fewer tokens: ~27% on FrontierMath took ~43M output tokens with o4-mini (Apr 2025) and ~5M with GPT-5.2 (Dec 2025).

Epoch's 2026 report measures cost per question, so it can't be joined to the per-token chart above. It also gives no confidence interval, and other methods give 43–58% a quarter. Epoch dates the starting o3 point to Jan 2025 (o3-mini's launch); o3 itself launched in Apr 2025, and we use that date.

Counting honestly

  • Enterprise spend is growing fast, but the yardstick moves. Menlo Ventures puts spending on using AI at a $176.8B annual rate in Sep 2026, against $33B for 2025. Its new definition adds frontier-model inference and software converting to AI, and each edition restates the past (2024: $13.8B → $11.5B → $8.5B), so the ~5× isn't like-for-like.
  • The "95% of pilots fail" study measured something narrow. MIT NANDA (Jul 2025) found no measurable P&L impact ~6 months after task-specific enterprise pilots, from 52 interviews and 153 survey responses. Generic chatbots went from pilot to deployment ~83% of the time. The report calls itself preliminary.
  • Adoption isn't a straight line. Ramp's index sat at 43.5–44.5% from Aug to Dec 2025, and OpenAI's share of businesses has been flat at ~40% since Jul 2025. Census large-firm use fell from 14.9% to 8.7% in mid-2025 before recovering. Workers' use of AI at work fell from 45.2% to 40.5% in Q3 2026.
  • The latest Census point needs a caveat. Since the 19 Oct collection, the question asks about "Super Intelligence or SI (formerly referred to as Artificial Intelligence or AI)", under an executive order. In that first wave, "don't know" rose from 9.6% to 13.9%.
  • Disclosures are getting thinner. Microsoft stopped giving token totals after FY25 and skipped its seat update in Oct 2026. Meta hasn't updated its user count since Jul 2025, OpenAI hasn't given an API token rate since Oct 2025, and Google's monthly total stops in May 2026.

  • Software comes first. Computer & maths work is ~34% of Claude.ai conversations and ~46% of API traffic. The single most common API task is fixing bugs in code, about 1 in 10 records.

What it says about the build-out

  • The market is pricing the future, not today. Rental rates rose through the same months that AI equities sold off and CoreWeave's CDS hit distress. As Silicon Data put it on 6 Nov: the fear is about "the odds of what will happen in the next couple years," not current demand.
  • Old chips are earning more, not less. A three-year-old H100 rents for ~50% more than a year ago, and SemiAnalysis reports 4-year H100 renewals at close to the original rates. That supports the 5–6-year depreciation the hyperscalers use and cuts against the "GPUs are worthless in 2 years" argument, at least for Hopper.
  • Payback is back above the hurdle (our inference). Latent Space's 2024 model (~$50k all-in per H100, 5-year life) loses money below ~$1.65/hr and clears ~10% IRR at ~$2.85. The late-2025 trough of $1.70–2.00 sat near the loss line. Today's ~$3 clears the hurdle.
  • What would flip it: Rubin-class price-performance (new chips gain ~49%/yr in performance per dollar, per Epoch) resetting Hopper rates; a wave of capacity landing in 2027; or lab revenue slowing. Early tell: GB200 on-demand medians fell 17% in one week (thin sample). Watch for the indices rolling over.

Verdict by company

  • CoreWeave: yes, blown out. Its spread went from ~855 bps (late Jul) to above 1,000 in mid-October, then to a record above 1,289 on 5 Nov before easing to ~1,155 (Deutsche Bank via CNBC). That implies ~60% risk-neutral odds of default within 5 years. Its four dollar bonds ($7.75B face) trade around 80 cents, yielding ~15–16%. Q3 results on 10 Nov are the next test. It carries more than $51B of debt and obligations, financed against GPU contracts. The ~$104B backlog is the offset, but lenders want operating cash flow, not promises.
  • Oracle: widening, not distressed. It is still rated investment grade (BBB- since S&P's July cut; Fitch and Moody's affirmed on 2 Nov), but its CDS prices like a BB/B credit at ~290–305. At its 28 Oct Investor Day it capped outside funding for FY27–30 at $20B, and the spread fell 22 bps. It then raised all $20B at once: $10B of stock at an 11% discount and $10B of bonds at 7.0–8.65%. The shares fell ~18% in three days. It has ~$125B of debt, and roughly half its $664B backlog is tied to OpenAI. It is the hinge between the neoclouds and big tech.
  • Hyperscalers and Nvidia: records, but from a tiny base. ~100 bps is ~8% risk-neutral 5-year odds, which is still strong investment grade. Much of the move is equity investors buying cheap downside hedges on the AI trade. CreditSights calls it "80% driven by technicals and sentiment." The new bond supply (~$67B since 29 Oct) also pushes spreads wider. Meta's equity raise helped for a week. It was reported as $95B, but the filings show a $25B forward share sale (cash only on settlement, by Nov 2027) and ~$12.5B of mandatory convertibles closed on 29 Oct. Its spread is at a record again, and S&P moved its outlook to negative, citing free operating cash flow deficits of $39B in 2026 and $71B in 2027.
  • Other neoclouds (Nebius, IREN, Applied Digital, Lambda, Crusoe): no liquid single-name CDS shows up in reporting. Watch their bond and convertible yields and share prices instead.

Signals that would mean real contagion

  • Oracle above ~400 bps, or a downgrade to junk. Investment-grade-only funds would be forced to sell.
  • Hyperscaler spreads above ~150–200 bps alongside capex guidance cuts, rather than just hedging volume.
  • CoreWeave failing to refinance, or its customers (Microsoft, OpenAI, Meta) renegotiating contracts.
  • Data-centre ABS and private-credit marks widening together with the public names.

The 5 Nov selloff. After the 3 Nov midterms, where Texas elected a governor who ran on a data-centre moratorium, the Nasdaq fell 5.5%, its worst day since April 2025. Nvidia fell 9%, CoreWeave 10%, AMD and Micron ~17%, Nebius and IREN 15–17%. CoreWeave's CDS hit its record that day, but the broad investment-grade CDX index moved only 59 → 62 bps, and the Nasdaq 100 rebounded ~2% on 6 Nov. So far this is equity and single-name stress, not a credit-market event.

Equity-side warning, 6 Nov: OpenAI's new round came in smaller and cheaper than planned (~$20B at $1.25T, against a $30B target at $1.4–1.5T). Anthropic's IPO talk fell from ~$2T to $1.0–1.5T. The labs owe most of the neocloud and Oracle backlog, so if equity gets harder to raise, those counterparties look weaker.

How circular is it?

  • It's real, and it's growing. Compute vendors put up ~66% of OpenAI's $122B round (Amazon $50B, Nvidia ~$30B reported) and ~23% of Anthropic's $65B Series H. Nvidia holds $99B of equity stakes, including $21.0B of SpaceX, $4.7B of CoreWeave and >5% of Nscale, which all sell compute to the labs. It has $108.5B of guarantees outstanding.
  • But it's a minority of the money. Analysts put the circular share of Nvidia's revenue at under 15% (Goldman, for 2027) or up to 13% (UBS, the OpenAI deal against 2026 estimates). The most aggressive view, New Street's, had Nvidia investing $10B for every $35B of GPUs OpenAI buys.
  • The revenue behind it is real. Microsoft booked $24.1B of revenue from OpenAI in FY26, against $13B invested. Anthropic booked $33.2B of revenue in Jan–Sep 2026.
  • Much of the vendor money only moves on delivery. Amazon's, Google's and AMD's 2026 money for Anthropic is released as capacity ships, AMD's OpenAI warrant hasn't vested, and Nvidia's lease guarantee starts with each lease. The paper gains are large (Amazon carries its $18B Anthropic stake at $238.7B), but they're set by funding rounds the vendors help fund.

Where it bites

  • Queues are a wish list, not a pipeline. Texas's grid operator had ~474 GW of large-load requests in late Jul 2026, ~90% of them data centres, against ~8.9 GW approved to energise (Jun). PJM expects 21.5 GW of data-centre load by 2028/29, up from 5.0 GW in service.
  • Turbines are sold out for years. Siemens Energy has a 69 GW gas-turbine backlog and lead times of three years or more. Wood Mackenzie counts 110 GW of orders against 60–70 GW a year of factory capacity, and expects turbine prices of ~$600/kW by end-2027 (+195% vs 2019).
  • Bills are rising, modestly so far. PJM's wholesale cost was $114.50/MWh in Jan–Jun 2026, against $103.40 without data-centre effects (market monitor, +11%). The US average residential price was 18.16¢/kWh in Aug 2026, +3.4% YoY (EIA).
  • Politics has turned. Texas: Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott on 3 Nov after calling for a data-centre moratorium (there's no evidence it decided the race). Abbott had already paused new data-centre environmental permits pending audits (21 Sep). 40 of 41 local ballot measures went against data centres (CNBC, from Ballotpedia's list). Data Center Watch counts ~$130B (Q1) and ~$68B (Q2) of projects blocked or delayed, but publishes no method.
  • Chips aren't off the hook. The IEA also names high-bandwidth memory shortages as a bottleneck for new data centres.

How to read the AI trade

  • Hyperscaler capex growth sped up. Big 4 capex roughly doubled from 2016 to 2021, an ordinary cloud cycle. Then it went ~$155B (2023) → ~$410B (2025) → ~$730B (2026). That is about 5× in three years.
  • Spending is now a meaningful slice of the economy. At ~$0.7–0.8T/yr, it is roughly 2.5% of US GDP, comparable to peak railroad or telecom build-outs.
  • The neoclouds and Oracle carry the leverage. Their combined backlog ($770B+) is concentrated in a few AI labs, and they rely more on debt than the Big 4. Oracle has capped new outside funding at $20B through FY30 and raised all of it in early November.
  • The cash is running out before the spending does. Big 4 capex took 99% of operating cash flow in Jan–Sep 2026, against 64% in 2025 and ~30–50% before that, and their free cash flow fell 97%. Amazon and Oracle now spend more than their businesses bring in. Alphabet and Meta stopped buybacks and are selling shares, and another ~$1.3T of leases is signed but not yet on the books. The offsets: operating cash flow is still up ~17%, and the spend sits on ~$1.8T of signed backlog.
  • AI changed the slope of the gigawatt curve. Global data-centre capacity added only ~7 GW from 2015 to 2021, while efficiency gains absorbed cloud growth. Since 2023 it has added ~15–25 GW per year, almost all of it AI. By 2030, AI is ~70% of a ~220 GW footprint.
  • Power and permits are the tightest constraint. PJM's capacity auction has cleared at its price cap three times running, ~11× the 2024/25 price and still 6.8 GW short, and its market monitor puts ~46% of the cost on data centres. GE Vernova has 122 GW of gas turbines ordered or reserved, with slots now going to 2032. Delays at third-party capacity are why Microsoft trimmed its guide. Politics is now part of it: Texas elected a governor who called for a data-centre moratorium, and 40 of 41 local ballot measures went against data centres. Memory chips are the other bottleneck. GW of contracted, permitted power is the metric to track.
  • Lab revenue is the hinge for the whole trade. OpenAI and Anthropic together run at ~$115B a year (Anthropic's filed Q3 revenue alone annualises to ~$68B), against ~$730B of 2026 Big 4 capex and $770B+ of neocloud and Oracle backlog that is mostly owed by these labs. Each has committed roughly 13× its annual revenue to compute (Anthropic: $895B in its S-1/A; OpenAI: >$665B). Revenue grows 4× to 13× a year now. If it slows toward 1.5×, the build-out looks over-extended. If it holds at 2–3×, the build-out looks undersized.
  • Private investors are getting pickier, but not leaving. OpenAI's Nov 2026 round was cut to ~$20B at $1.25T, from a $30B target at $1.4–1.5T. Anthropic's IPO talk fell from ~$2T to $1.0–1.5T. The valuation-to-run-rate multiple has fallen from ~35–38× in late 2025 to ~20–25×, mostly because revenue grew faster than valuations, not because valuations fell. The risk is the funding gap: OpenAI's >$665B of compute commitments are many times what any single round now raises.
  • Circular, but only partly. Vendors' firm money into OpenAI and Anthropic (~$134B) is ~12% of the ~$1.15T of firm compute the two have committed to buy, or ~24% counting guarantees and conditional stakes. Most of the bill has to be paid from revenue and outside capital. The circular part is growing, though: Nvidia's equity purchases were 24% of its revenue in Feb–Jul 2026, and vendors supplied ~66% of OpenAI's last big round.
  • Usage is broad, and you can count it. ChatGPT has 1.2B weekly users, Google processed >3.2 quadrillion tokens a month in May 2026 (~330× in two years), 56% of US businesses on Ramp pay for AI and 24% of firms tell the Census they use it. Adoption has plateaus, though, and the companies are disclosing less.
  • Autonomy compounds, and so does token demand. The length of task an AI agent can finish on its own doubled every ~4 months from 2023 until METR's benchmark ran out at ~17 hours in 2026, and METR now puts overall progress at about twice the 2024–25 pace. Models match or beat experts on ~85% of GDPval's work samples but complete only ~21% of real freelance projects. Hours-long agent runs consume orders of magnitude more tokens than a chat reply. That is why falling token prices coexist with rising compute demand.
  • The fundamentals and the market disagree. GPU rental rates are rising for every generation: H100 +50% since Dec 2025, B200 +50% YTD, with list-price hikes at AWS, Lambda, Nebius and RunPod. Equity and credit stress is about whether that demand lasts through the next wave of capacity, not about today's pricing. A rollover in the rental indices would be the first real sign of oversupply. The 5 Nov selloff was driven by politics and sentiment; the rental indices hadn't turned as of 6 Nov.
  • The credit market is drawing the line at leverage, not at AI. CoreWeave's CDS hit a record above 1,289 bps on 5 Nov (distress) and Oracle's prices like junk. Meta, Nvidia and the other hyperscalers sit at records, but only ~50–140 bps, and the broad IG index barely moved. The stress is in debt-funded capacity with concentrated customers, not yet in the Big 4, although they now borrow and sell shares too (see the cash-flow card).
  • Tokens deflate while capex inflates. Holding capability fixed, the cost falls roughly 5× to 100× a year depending on the task and method (Epoch's 2026 estimate: ~13×/yr). Frontier launch prices don't fall that way ($4–20 per 1M tokens for the Sep–Oct 2026 flagships), and newer models use more tokens per task. Cheaper intelligence widens the use cases. That is the bull case for the spend, but it also squeezes margins for anyone selling commodity tokens.
  • Compute keeps compounding, with a caveat. Frontier training runs grew ~4–5×/yr through 2024. Epoch's newest estimate (GPT-6 Astra, ~9.4e26) sits below that trend, although most 2026 models have no estimate yet. Sustaining that to 2030 means 1–5 GW training clusters, which is what the campuses above are for.
