    How the gap is being filled

    • Buybacks have stopped at two of them. Alphabet (2025: $45.7B) and Meta (2025: $26.2B) bought back no stock in Jan–Sep 2026. Amazon doesn't buy back stock, and Microsoft's buybacks have slowed a little.
    • They're selling shares. Alphabet raised $42.9B of common stock and $19.1B of mandatory convertibles in Jan–Sep 2026, for "capital expenditures to scale AI infrastructure". Meta agreed a $25B forward share sale (cash on settlement by Nov 2027) and ~$12.5B of mandatory convertibles. Oracle raised ~$35B of equity across convertibles, at-the-market sales and a $10B offering.
    • And borrowing. In Jan–Sep 2026, Amazon raised $115.1B of long-term debt, Alphabet $85.2B and Meta $24.9B. Microsoft sold $25B of bonds on 29 Oct, its first in nine years. Meta followed with $15B of dollar bonds and a record €9B euro debut (3–4 Nov), and Alphabet with €6B (2 Nov). Amazon (−$66B) and Oracle (−$88B) now have net debt, and Meta is about even.
    • Off balance sheet too. Meta's Hyperion joint venture with Blue Owl (~$27B) and a ~$14B Texas venture leave it with up to $68.4B of exposure, mostly residual-value guarantees. Alphabet guarantees $48.0B of other companies' data-centre leases and power contracts, up from $16.9B at end-2025.

    What the forecasts say, and the counter-case

    • Free cash flow is expected to go negative. S&P Global Ratings expects all six hyperscalers (including Oracle and SpaceX) to have negative free operating cash flow in 2026 and 2027, recovering only in 2029. BofA's estimate for eight big spenders is −$64B (2026), −$144B (2027) and −$186B (2028). Amazon's own release: free cash flow "decreased to an outflow of $42.4 billion for the trailing twelve months".
    • Microsoft is the exception. It expects to stay free-cash-flow positive in FY27, and it still has net cash.
    • Oracle has the tightest path. It targets positive free cash flow in 2H FY28 and $30B+ in FY29, with capex peaking in FY27–28 and growth "self-funded beginning in FY29" (Investor Day, 28 Oct).
    • The counter-case. Operating cash flow is still growing fast (Big 4: $677B in the latest 12 months vs $581B in 2025). The spend is backed by ~$1.8T of signed cloud backlog, and rising GPU rental rates support the 5–6-year server lives. Amazon has been here before: in 2021–22 its capex ran at 120–125% of operating cash flow during a warehouse build-out, and then it fell back.

    What a gigawatt means

    • 1 GW of power runs roughly 750k–850k US homes.
    • 1 GW of AI capacity is about 500k–600k Blackwell-class GPUs, all-in.
    • All-in capex is roughly $35–50B per GW (chips, building, power).
    • ~110 GW of new AI capacity by 2030 implies ~$4–5T of build-out.

    Why it matters for the build-out

    • Any given capability gets cheaper by roughly 5× to 100× a year, much faster than Moore's law. The estimates differ by method: Epoch 13×/yr (2026), MIT FutureTech 5–10×/yr, Artificial Analysis 18–123× in about a year depending on the score.
    • The cost of frontier capability does not fall this way. Every new top model resets the price bar (below).
    • Cheaper tokens drive more usage, including agents and long reasoning chains. That demand is what fills the gigawatts above.

    Cheaper per token, more tokens per task

    • Reasoning multiplies tokens. Reasoning models use ~8× the tokens of older models on the same questions, and their output length grows ~5× a year (Epoch). Claude 3.7 Sonnet used 98M output tokens to run Artificial Analysis's index with thinking on, against 6.3M with it off.
    • Better scores still cost more. The top Artificial Analysis index score rose from 21 to 58 in a year, at ~11× the cost per task (20 Oct 2026). Claude Fable 5.2 has since taken it to 65, at the highest cost per task in the table. Running frontier models is getting 3–18× more expensive a year (MIT FutureTech), even as each fixed level gets cheaper.
    • Near-frontier at a fraction of the cost (Nov 2026). GPT-6.2 Astra scores 62 on Artificial Analysis's index for $4.00 a task, and Gemini 4 Neon 61 for $6.44. The leader, Claude Fable 5.2, scores 65 for $26.54, so the last three or four points cost 4–7×. GPT-6.2 Astra has the same list price as Fable 5.2 ($8 / $40); the difference is tokens: ~66M output tokens to run the index against Fable's 275M. On Epoch's ECI the two are 0.6 apart (173.4 and 172.8); Epoch hasn't scored Neon yet.
    • September's flagship level reached the cheap tier in ten weeks. Gemini 4 Hydrogen (12 Nov, $1.50 / $7.50, with a free tier) scores 54, above Claude Fable 5.1 (49), which launched on 1 Sep at $10 / $50. Its cost per task is about a quarter of Fable 5.1's.     • Queues are a wish list, not a pipeline. PJM expects 21.5 GW of data-centre load by 2028/29, up from 5.0 GW in service.
    • Turbines are sold out for years. Siemens Energy ended its fiscal year (30 Sep) with 104 GW of gas-turbine commitments: 65 GW of firm backlog (69 GW at 30 Jun, as it shipped 6 GW against ~2 GW of new orders) plus 39 GW of slot reservations, up from ~26 GW, that it expects to firm up in FY27. It reports ~€15B of its record €164B backlog as data-centre related, and still says attractive delivery windows "command a premium, especially in the United States" (results, 11 Nov). GE Vernova is at 122 GW (64 GW firm).     How to read the AI trade

    • Hyperscaler capex growth sped up. Big 4 capex roughly doubled from 2016 to 2021, an ordinary cloud cycle. Then it went ~$155B (2023) → ~$410B (2025) → ~$730B (2026). That is about 5× in three years.
    • Spending is now a meaningful slice of the economy. At ~$0.7–0.8T/yr, it is roughly 2.5% of US GDP, comparable to peak railroad or telecom build-outs.
    • The neoclouds and Oracle carry the leverage. Their combined backlog ($770B+) is concentrated in a few AI labs, and they rely more on debt than the Big 4. Oracle has capped new outside funding at $20B through FY30 and raised all of it in early November.
    • The cash is running out before the spending does. Big 4 capex took 99% of operating cash flow in Jan–Sep 2026, against 64% in 2025 and ~30–50% before that, and their free cash flow fell 97%. Amazon and Oracle now spend more than their businesses bring in. Alphabet and Meta stopped buybacks and are selling shares, and another ~$1.3T of leases is signed but not yet on the books. The offsets: operating cash flow is still up ~17%, and the spend sits on ~$1.8T of signed backlog.
    • AI changed the slope of the gigawatt curve. Global data-centre capacity added only ~7 GW from 2015 to 2021, while efficiency gains absorbed cloud growth. Since 2023 it has added ~15–25 GW per year, almost all of it AI. By 2030, AI is ~70% of a ~220 GW footprint.
    • Power and permits are the tightest constraint. PJM's capacity auction has cleared at its price cap three times running, ~11× the 2024/25 price and still 6.8 GW short, and its market monitor puts ~46% of the cost on data centres. GE Vernova has 122 GW of gas turbines ordered or reserved, with slots now going to 2032. Delays at third-party capacity are why Microsoft trimmed its guide. Politics is now part of it: Texas elected a governor who called for a data-centre moratorium, and 40 of 41 local ballot measures went against data centres. Memory chips are the other bottleneck. GW of contracted, permitted power is the metric to track.
    • Lab revenue is the hinge for the whole trade. OpenAI and Anthropic together run at ~$130B a year (Anthropic's prospectus gives ~$81B for October; its filed Q3 revenue annualises to ~$68B), against ~$730B of 2026 Big 4 capex and $770B+ of neocloud and Oracle backlog that is mostly owed by these labs. Each has committed roughly 13× its annual revenue to compute (Anthropic: $900.9B in its S-1/A; OpenAI: >$665B). Revenue grows 4× to 13× a year now. If it slows toward 1.5×, the build-out looks over-extended. If it holds at 2–3×, the build-out looks undersized.
    • Private investors are getting pickier, but not leaving. OpenAI's Nov 2026 round has raised $11–15B at $1.25T, against a $30B target at $1.4–1.5T. Anthropic's IPO priced above its range at $83 on 18 Nov, ~$1.1T on shares outstanding against ~$2T talked about a month earlier, and raised $81B plus $26B from suppliers, the largest IPO on record (~$93.5B once the banks took up their option). After closing day one up 17% ($97.17), it slipped below its $83 IPO price on 24 Nov ($81.91) and closed at $82.79 on 25 Nov. The valuation-to-run-rate multiple has fallen from ~35–38× in late 2025 to ~25× for OpenAI and ~14–19× for Anthropic at its IPO price, mostly because revenue grew faster than valuations, not because valuations fell. The risk is the funding gap: OpenAI's >$665B of compute commitments are many times what any single round now raises.
    • Circular, but only partly. Vendors' firm money into OpenAI and Anthropic (~$159B) is ~14% of the ~$1.15T of firm compute the two have committed to buy, or ~25% counting guarantees and conditional stakes. Most of the bill has to be paid from revenue and outside capital. The circular part is growing, though: Nvidia's equity purchases were 24% of its revenue in Feb–Jul 2026, and vendors supplied ~66% of OpenAI's last big round.
    • Usage is broad, and you can count it. ChatGPT has 1.2B weekly users, Google processed >3.2 quadrillion tokens a month in May 2026 (~330× in two years), 57% of US businesses on Ramp pay for AI and 25% of firms tell the Census they use it. Adoption has plateaus, though, and the companies are disclosing less.
    • Autonomy compounds, and so does token demand. The length of task an AI agent can finish on its own doubled every ~4 months from 2023 until METR's benchmark ran out at ~17 hours in 2026. On METR's harder suite (20 Nov), the best models tested reach ~40 hours with very wide error bars, and METR puts overall progress at about twice the 2024–25 pace. Models match or beat experts on ~85% of GDPval's work samples and complete ~41% of real freelance projects, up from ~21% before Claude Fable 5.2 launched on 9 Nov. Hours-long agent runs consume orders of magnitude more tokens than a chat reply. That is why falling token prices coexist with rising compute demand.
    • The fundamentals and the market disagree. GPU rental rates are rising for every generation: H100 +54% since Dec 2025, B200 +59% YTD, B300 +60% since its April launch, with list-price hikes at AWS, Lambda, Nebius and RunPod. Equity and credit stress is about whether that demand lasts through the next wave of capacity, not about today's pricing. A rollover in the rental indices would be the first real sign of oversupply. Even as credit spreads hit records on 25 Nov, the Silicon Data indices still hadn't turned: H100 ($3.08) is ~2% off its 14 Nov high, B200 ($6.99) is ~1.5% off its high, B300 set a new high ($7.72), and Ornn's H100 rebounded to $3.16 on 24 Nov after its brief dip. The rental market and the bond market are telling different stories.
    • The credit market is drawing the line at leverage, not at AI. CoreWeave's CDS hit a new record of 1,314 bps on 25 Nov (S&P GMI; ICE reportedly printed 1,534) with 2031 bond yields near 18%, and Oracle's reached a record 356 bps (priced like junk). Nvidia touched an intraday record 147.3 bps on 25 Nov as S&P placed its AA rating on CreditWatch Negative over governance, and Meta and Alphabet sit at records too, but only ~95–147 bps. The stress is sharpest in debt-funded capacity with concentrated customers, not yet in the Big 4, although they now borrow and sell shares too (see the cash-flow card).
    • Tokens deflate while capex inflates. Holding capability fixed, the cost falls roughly 5× to 100× a year depending on the task and method (Epoch's 2026 estimate: ~13×/yr). Frontier launch prices don't fall that way ($4–20 per 1M tokens for the Sep–Nov 2026 flagships), and newer models use more tokens per task. Cheaper intelligence widens the use cases. That is the bull case for the spend, but it also squeezes margins for anyone selling commodity tokens.
    • Compute keeps compounding, with a caveat. Frontier training runs grew ~4–5×/yr through 2024. Epoch's newest estimate (GPT-6 Astra, ~9.4e26) sits below that trend, although most 2026 models have no estimate yet. Sustaining that to 2030 means 1–5 GW training clusters, which is what the campuses above are for.
